Bomb Calorimetry

Burn a weighed organic solid in high-pressure oxygen at constant volume, measure the temperature rise of the surrounding water, and turn it into the energy, enthalpy and enthalpy of formation of the compound.

Part A

Principles of bomb calorimetry

Why a sealed steel vessel gives the internal energy of combustion, how the calorimeter is calibrated, and how the raw temperature record is turned into a reliable ΔT.

A.1Heat at constant volume

A bomb calorimeter burns a weighed sample in excess oxygen inside a sealed, thick-walled steel vessel called the bomb. The bomb's volume cannot change, so the reacting system does no pressure–volume work on its surroundings. The first law of thermodynamics then links the heat released directly to the change in internal energy of the reaction.4

ΔU = q + w,   w = −∫pext dV = 0  ⇒  ΔU = qV(1)

The heat released by the reaction flows through the bomb wall into about 2 kg of water that surrounds it. We measure the temperature rise of that water, ΔT, with a resolution of 0.001 K. If we know how much energy warms the whole assembly by one kelvin, ΔT tells us qV.

A.2The energy balance

The bomb, the bucket of water, the stirrer and the temperature probe together form the calorimeter. Its effective heat capacity Ccal is the energy needed to raise the whole assembly by 1 K; for a standard plain-jacket instrument with 2000 g of water it is close to 10.1 kJ K−1.1 Treating the calorimeter and its contents as isolated, all the energy released inside appears as a temperature rise:

Ccal ΔT = −m Δcu + ewire + eacid(2)

Here m is the sample mass and Δcu the specific energy of combustion (J g−1, negative). Two small energy sources that are not from the sample must be included:1,2

  • Fuse wire, ewire. The Ni–Cr ignition wire partly burns. For Parr 45C10 wire, use 9.6 J per cm burned (2.3 cal cm−1).
  • Nitric acid, eacid. N2 left from the air in the bomb is partly oxidized to HNO3, which releases energy. Titrating the bomb washings with 0.0709 N Na2CO3 gives 4.184 J (1 cal) per mL of titrant.

Ccal cannot be calculated reliably from the masses and specific heats of the parts, so it is measured by burning a primary standard. Benzoic acid is used because it is non-hygroscopic, burns cleanly and is certified: NIST SRM 39j has Δcu = −26.434 kJ g−1 under bomb conditions.5

Ccal = mBA(26 434 J g−1) + ewire + eacidΔT(3)

With Ccal known, the same balance solved for an unknown sample gives its specific and molar energy of combustion:

Δcu = − Ccal ΔT − ewire − eacidm(4)
ΔcUm = M · Δcu(5)

A.3From internal energy to enthalpy

Thermochemical tables report enthalpies. Since H = U + pV, the two differ by Δ(pV) for the reaction. The volumes of solids and liquids are negligible, and gases behave nearly ideally, so only the change in the number of moles of gas, Δng, matters.4 Water is counted as a liquid, because the bomb atmosphere is saturated with water vapor before firing.

ΔcH = ΔcU + ΔngRT(6)

A.4Enthalpy of formation by Hess's law

Combustion links the compound to CO2(g) and H2O(l), whose standard enthalpies of formation are known very precisely (−393.51 and −285.83 kJ mol−1).11 Going from the elements to the products by either route must give the same enthalpy change, so the enthalpy of formation of the compound follows.

ΔfH°(CaHbOc) = a ΔfH°(CO2, g) + b2 ΔfH°(H2O, l) − ΔcH°(7)

ΔfH° is a small difference between two large numbers. An error of 0.1 % in ΔcH (about 5 kJ mol−1 for naphthalene) becomes an error of several percent in ΔfH°.

A.5Reading the thermogram

The temperature record of a run has three parts. In the pre-period the water drifts slowly because of stirring and heat exchange with the jacket. After firing, the temperature rises steeply for 2–3 minutes as heat moves through the bomb wall. In the post-period it drifts slowly again, usually now downward, because the water is warmer than the jacket.

Heat is exchanged with the jacket during the rise too, so the plain difference between final and initial temperatures is not the true adiabatic ΔT. The standard correction, due to Dickinson,8 fits straight lines to the pre- and post-period drifts and extrapolates both to the time t60 at which 60 % of the rise has occurred.2,3 The vertical gap between the lines at that time is the corrected rise:

ΔT = Tpost(t60) − Tpre(t60)(8)

Assumptions to keep in mind
  • Combustion is complete: all carbon ends as CO2 and all hydrogen as liquid water. Soot in the capsule means the run is invalid.
  • Ccal is the same in every run. Use the same bomb, the same water mass (2000.0 g) and the same starting temperature range.
  • Heat exchange with the jacket is proportional to the temperature difference (Newton's law), so linear drift extrapolation is valid.
  • The values obtained refer to bomb conditions. Small Washburn corrections convert them to standard states.7,9

    B.3Procedure

    The steps follow the manufacturer's instructions2,12 and the same general sequence as the standard method ASTM D5865.10

    Safety
    • Wear safety glasses throughout. Never fire a bomb that is not fully submerged in the bucket, or that leaks bubbles.
    • Do not exceed 30 atm of oxygen, or the energy limit of your bomb. The Parr 1108 vessel is rated for at most 8000 cal (33 kJ) per charge: about 1.2 g of benzoic acid or 0.8 g of naphthalene.12
    • Keep oil and grease away from oxygen fittings. Stand clear of the calorimeter for 20 s after firing.
    • Release pressure slowly, over about one minute, before opening the bomb.

    Prepare the sample

    1. Press a pellet of about 1.0 g of benzoic acid (calibration) or 0.7–0.8 g of sample. Brush off loose powder.
    2. Weigh the empty capsule, then the capsule with pellet, to ±0.0001 g. Record the pellet mass in the worksheet.

    Assemble and charge the bomb

    1. Thread a 10.0 cm piece of fuse wire between the electrodes. Bend it so it touches the top of the pellet but not the capsule.
    2. Pipette 1.0 mL of deionized water into the bomb cylinder.
    3. Place the head in the cylinder and screw the cap down hand-tight.
    4. Connect the oxygen line and fill slowly to 25–30 atm. Close the valve and disconnect.

    Set up the calorimeter

    1. Weigh 2000.0 g of water into the bucket, about 1–2 K below room temperature.
    2. Place the bucket in the jacket, lower the bomb in, and check for a stream of bubbles (a leak). Attach the ignition leads.
    3. Close the cover, lower the probe, and start the stirrer. Let it run 5 min to reach a steady drift.

    Record the run

    1. Record the temperature every 30 s for 5 min (pre-period).
    2. At exactly 5.0 min, press and hold the firing button until the indicator goes out (about 5 s).
    3. Keep recording every 30 s through the rise and for at least 5 min after the rate of change becomes steady (post-period), to about 20 min.

    After firing

    1. Stop the stirrer, lift out the bomb and vent it slowly through the valve.
    2. Open the bomb and inspect the capsule. If there is soot or unburned sample, discard the run.
    3. Remove the unburned wire, straighten it and measure its length.
    4. Rinse all inner surfaces with deionized water, collect the washings and titrate with 0.0709 N Na2CO3. Record the volume.
    5. Dry the bomb. Repeat for a second benzoic acid run and two sample runs.
